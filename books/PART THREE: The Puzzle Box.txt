PART THREE: CONVERGENCE
The Puzzle Box
---
Chapter 11: Offcuts
The forensic lab in Salt Lake had a window on the third floor that looked out over a field of wild grass and a cluster of tin-roofed huts where the cleaners lived. Inspector Sen had been sitting by it for two hours. The grass was yellow in the afternoon light. A goat stood in it and chewed slowly, looking at nothing, and every so often a crow landed on the goat's back and rode it for a few steps before flying off.
On the desk in front of Sen lay two evidence bags, one from Kolkata, one from Asansol, and a stack of printed reports two inches thick. He had read every page. He had read them twice.
The metallurgist came in at four with a cup of tea in one hand and a folder in the other. Her name was Dr. Sanjukta Paul, and she had worked on the Howrah ammunition case and the Barasat chemical fire, and she had the flat, unhurried manner of someone who had long ago stopped expecting the world to be tidy.
"You'll want the short version," she said, sitting down across from him.
"I want the true version."
"That's the same thing, usually. The short version is worse." She opened the folder. "The collar casing from Kolkata is tool steel. Not commercial. Not off a rack. It's machined from a plate that matches the armour plate from the Asansol machine, down to the alloy batch. Same mill. Same heat treatment. Same burr pattern on the lathe cuts."
"Explain the burr pattern."
"Every lathe leaves a signature. Feed rate, tool wear, the way the operator holds the cut. Your Kolkata collar was turned on a lathe whose bed has a small chip in the left-hand ways. Thirty microns, nothing you'd see. But the Asansol machine's offcuts have the same chip in the same place, and the same double tooling mark on the bore, where the operator changed his bit halfway through and didn't re-zero."
"So the same man turned both."
"Or the same man taught someone else to turn them. The mark's the same. The hand may not be." She sipped her tea. "The welding wire is also consistent. The low-hydrogen electrode, the flux, the batch code on the spool. It's from the same supplier in Howrah. Sold to Asansol Silencer Works in March last year, and to a shop in Garden Reach, under a different name, the same month."
Sen wrote it down. "Garden Reach."
"Near the transmission tower on the Kolkata side. It's on the tower's property boundary, according to the survey maps. The shop is a washing-machine repair place, registered to a B. Chakraborty."
Sen looked up.
"And the timers," Dr. Paul said. "Two kitchen timers, the round red kind. From the batch I've seen in this building for three years. Imported components, assembled in a factory in Hooghly. The batch number's stamped on the back of the dial, and it's been partially scratched off. Someone tried to remove it with a file." She turned a photograph round and slid it across the desk. "They didn't finish. The last three digits are still there."
Sen studied the photograph. He did not need to be told what the digits meant. He had seen them before, on an invoice for a box of cheap timers sold to a repair shop in Garden Reach, in a file he had requested three days ago and not yet opened.
"There's one more thing," Dr. Paul said. She picked up a small plastic evidence bag and turned it over. Inside, a single grey granule of dust clung to the plastic. "Found in the seam of the Kolkata collar. Coal dust. Not the local kind. High sulphur, high volatiles. Raniganj seam."
"The Asansol machine?"
"The Asansol machine was sitting in a coal pit for two years before the shop bought it. The dust is in every crack of its cab. Same seam." She set the bag down. "Whoever built the collar had been in a room with coal dust on their hands. And the shop in Garden Reach doesn't stock coal."
Sen looked out the window again. The goat had moved to the edge of the field. The crow had gone. A cleaner in a faded sari was sweeping the path with a broom made of coconut fibre, slow strokes, raising a cloud of yellow dust that drifted in the late light and settled on the wet grass.
"Doctor," he said. "Did someone tell the bank manager they were going to rob his strongroom?"
"No idea." Dr. Paul stood and tucked the folder under her arm. "But whoever it was, they knew exactly how much cash the morning run would be carrying. Eighty bundles. Five-hundreds. I checked the depot manifest for the Asansol cooperative this morning. The manifest has the same batch of coal dust written in the margins. Someone at the depot is careless with his pen."
She left him to the window.
Sen sat for a long time without moving. Then he picked up the phone and dialled the number of the Kolkata Municipal Corporation's Garden Reach ward office, and asked for the records of a repair shop registered to B. Chakraborty, and when the clerk on the other end asked him why he wanted to know, he said, very quietly, "Because a man I arrested in a parking lot three weeks ago told me a woman liked timetables. And I've just found the handwriting."

Chapter 12: The Freezer in Garden Reach
The house on the lane behind the washing-machine shop was a squat grey box with an iron gate painted green, a line of dead bougainvillea on its boundary wall, and a garage door at the side that had not been opened in six weeks. A neighbour's dog lay across the lane in the shade, watching the police vans with long-suffering patience. The air smelled of rotting flowers, damp concrete, and something sweeter underneath that made the constables turn their heads and cough.
The search team had come in at dawn. By nine, they had found what they had been looking for in the back of the garage, behind a stack of rusted tyre rims and a broken lawnmower.
A chest freezer. Heavy, white, dented on one corner. Its plug had been pulled from the wall, and its lid was sealed with a band of electrician's tape.
Sen stood at the garage door and watched the forensic team lift the tape. The lid came up slowly. A breath of cold air rolled out over the concrete floor, grey and sharp, and with it a smell that made the nearest constable turn away and vomit into the drain.
Inside, folded into the freezer's bottom in a nest of ice-cracked plastic sheeting, lay the body of a man. He had been dead for some time. The freezer had kept him cold, and the cold had kept him from becoming a thing that anyone would look at for long. He was lying on his side, one knee drawn up, one arm folded beneath his head, as if asleep. There was a neat dark wound in the middle of his back.
"Jayanta Roy," said the sergeant, reading from a wallet taken from the dead man's trouser pocket. "Forty-four. Driving licence. Lived in Ballygunge."
"Who reported him missing?"
"Nobody." The sergeant closed the freezer lid with his knuckle. "Nobody reported him at all. That's what's strange."
Sen said nothing. He was looking at the tyre rims, the broken mower, the grey oil stains on the concrete beside a steel bench where a long shelf of tools hung on pegs. A row of lathe bits sat in a wooden box on the bench. Beside them lay a folded blue cloth, neatly ironed, and under the cloth, the edge of a single white card.
He lifted the cloth with two fingers.
A business card. Printed, cheap, yellow-edged. B. CHAKRABORTY. WASHING MACHINE & APPLIANCE REPAIR. FREE VISIT, SECOND VISIT NO CHARGE. Below the printed text, in blue biro, someone had written a single line.
Madhumita. Monday. Bring the envelope.
---
The woman was in an interrogation room on the fourth floor of Lalbazar, and she was talking before the door had closed behind Sen.
Madhumita Ghosh was forty-nine years old. She wore a silk blouse the colour of old wine, a long silver necklace, and dark reading glasses pushed up into hair that had been dyed a flat, unnatural black. Her nails were lacquered crimson. She was thin, almost fragile, but there was nothing fragile in the way she held herself, with her spine straight and her shoulders back, her chin lifted as though someone were measuring her for a portrait.
"Inspector Sen," she said. "How nice. I was told you would be handsome. I was told wrongly."
"Mrs. Ghosh."
"Oh, don't. It's Dutta, or Ghosh, or whatever the newspapers have decided. I have been Mrs. so many things." She smiled. It was a bright, sharp smile, and it did not stay in place. Her eyes moved over him, quick and furious. "You have been busy. Very busy. Two dead men and a bank full of frightened tellers. You must be exhausted."
Sen sat down across from her. He put a folder on the table. He opened it, slowly, and laid the first photograph face up.
It was a close-up of a freezer lid, taken in the garden of the house on the lane behind the washing-machine shop.
Madhumita glanced at it. The smile did not change. "Ah. Jayanta. He would have hated the photograph. He had a very vain streak about his feet."
"You knew him."
"I was engaged to him for four months. In 2002." She picked up a pen from the table, examined it, and set it down again. "He was a dull man with an interesting temper. He shot himself in the foot once, on purpose, to get out of a dinner party with my father. He failed. The bullet went through his sock and out the other side. He told everyone it was an accident."
"He was shot in the back, Mrs. Ghosh."
"Was he?" She tilted her head, feigning mild surprise. "How careless of somebody."
Sen laid the second photograph on the table. It showed a jar of glass, half-full of grey, dusty sediment, the rim sealed with red wax. A label on the glass, written in pencil, was smudged but legible.
Keep dry.
Madhumita's eyes flicked to it, and for the first time something moved behind the bright surface of her face.
"That's not mine," she said.
"It was found in your car," Sen said. "On the back seat, under a shawl. The same car you drove to the Doordarshan mast on the marsh road, on the afternoon of the twelfth of May. Four witnesses saw it parked there for forty minutes."
"I don't own a car, Inspector. I have a driver."
"Your driver is in the next room, Mrs. Ghosh. He has been very helpful."
For a moment, the room was perfectly silent. Madhumita's crimson nails tapped once against the table. Then she laughed. It was a high, clear, delighted sound, rising and rising until it cracked at the top.
"Oh, Kenny," she said. "Oh, you stupid little man."
Sen waited.
When the laughter stopped, Madhumita leaned back in her chair and folded her arms across her chest. Her face was calm again, and her voice had lost its warmth entirely.
"You want to know about the money," she said. "Two lakh fifty thousand. I wanted it to pay Kenny to put a bullet in my father's head. My father squandered the inheritance my grandfather left me. He let the bank sell the Ballygunge flat. He let the Rolex go to a moneylender in Burrabazar. He let the emeralds go to his second wife. Do you know what it is to be a woman whose entire life has been stolen by a man in a silk dressing gown?"
"I can imagine."
"No, you can't. Nobody can. That's the joke." She smiled at him, and her eyes were perfectly dry. "Kenny was going to do it in July. He had the gun. He wanted a deposit. I wanted him to be quick about it. And then Jayanta started talking, the way he always did when he had too much whisky. He told the Ballygunge club that Madhumita had a temper. That she had shot her own boyfriend's cousin in the leg in 1986. That she had a gun in her handbag. He said it at the club, Inspector, in front of a dozen men who knew my father."
"So you shot him."
"I shot him in the back," Madhumita said flatly. "With a shotgun. In the garage of a man who owed me a favour. And then I told that man to put him in the freezer, because I had nowhere else to put him, and because he was the only person in Kolkata who understood how much time it takes to clean up after a man."
"And who was that?"
Madhumita looked at him for a long moment. Her eyes narrowed.
"The handyman," she said. "The one who fixes washing machines. You've been to his shop. You've seen the card."
"Yes."
"Then you know who he is." She leaned forward, close, and her voice dropped to a murmur. "He's the cleverest man I have ever met, Inspector. He is a genius with lathes. He can turn a bar of tool steel into a lock you cannot pick with a torch and a lifetime. And he is dying. Did you know that? Lymphoma. His ankles are swollen. He cannot stand for more than twenty minutes. He sits in his garage at night with a bottle of cough syrup and a photograph of his dead wife, and he tells me that he will be gone before any of you can put a rope around his neck."
"And the freezer?"
"He put the freezer in the garage. Not me." Madhumita's lips curled. "He melted the shotgun, you know. In the furnace in his back shop. He took it apart piece by piece and threw the scraps into the marsh behind the tower, the way a man feeds the fish. I helped him measure the collar. I helped him choose the timers. That's all I did."
"And the collar?"
"The collar was his." She sat back again, pleased. "The bank was mine. The mast was his. The cats were Kenny's idea, you know. He thought they would make the victim more pliable. He was wrong. The victim was a good man."
Sen looked at her for a long moment without speaking.
"Mrs. Ghosh," he said finally. "Did Bimal Chakraborty ever mention a man named Banerjee? In Asansol?"
Madhumita's face went blank.
It was a small change, almost imperceptible. A shutter closing behind a window. But Sen had spent twenty years watching faces in interrogation rooms, and he knew the difference between a woman who had forgotten something and a woman who had just been handed a piece of the puzzle she had not known she was holding.
"Asansol?" she said slowly. "No. Why would he?"
"Because on the afternoon of the fourth of June, a machine built from his lathe work rolled through the centre of Asansol, and killed nobody, and put a library's worth of children into the street. And because the man who built it had a list. One hundred and seven names. Your name is not on it." Sen closed the folder. "But the dead man in the freezer is."
Madhumita's hand twitched on the table. She looked at it as though it belonged to someone else.
"No," she whispered. "He wouldn't. He's a handyman. He fixes washing machines."
Sen stood up.
"He's dead, Mrs. Ghosh," he said quietly. "Your handyman. He died in the early hours of the third of July, on a cot in the back room of his shop in Garden Reach, with the lights off. The doctor called it lymphoma. The neighbours called it a mercy."
He reached the door, then stopped, and looked back at her.
"He left a note."
Madhumita's face crumpled. For the first time since she had walked into the room, she looked her age, and older.

Chapter 13: This Has Nothing to Do With the Bank
The note was on the desk in the back room of Bimal Chakraborty's shop, under a glass paperweight shaped like a lotus, and it had been written in the same neat, careful hand as the typed directions in the bank, the pizza slip, the map on the Park Street pavement. It was dated the third of July, 2004. It was addressed to nobody.
Sen read it standing by the window in the late light, with a constable holding a torch over the page.
I was asked to explain my part in the matter of Jayanta Roy. I admit that I knew of his body in the freezer. I admit that I helped the woman move the shotgun into my furnace. I admit that I melted it. I admit that I was not fond of him.
I did not kill him. The woman did, and she shall be punished for it by people far less clever than myself.
This has nothing to do with the Hooghly Commercial Bank case.
Sen read the last line three times. He read the words beneath it, written smaller, in pencil, as if they had been an afterthought.
Do not look at the lathe.
He did not look at the lathe.
He looked instead at the desk, at the neat stacks of invoices held together with brass clips, at the pencil cup full of sharpened stubs, at the framed photograph of a young woman with a bright, open face and a wedding sari, standing in front of a wall painted with a mural of the Howrah Bridge. On the bottom shelf of the desk, under a tin of cough syrup, lay a cheap plastic tape recorder with its lid open and its batteries missing, and beside it a small, wooden box, pale and unvarnished, with a hinged lid and a brass clasp.
The clasp was open. Inside the box, cushioned on a bed of faded green baize, lay a single kitchen timer. Round. Red. Its cream dial was stopped at fifteen.
Sen stood very still.
Beside the box lay a folded map, two pages of it, creased and soft. The hand-drawn lines of the Grand Trunk Road curved across the paper toward a small red square at its edge, and in the margin, in pencil, someone had drawn a tiny stick figure with its arms raised, and a little speech balloon above its head.
Go on, Dada. Your mother's waiting up.
---
They brought Bimal Chakraborty's body to the morgue at Chittaranjan Hospital on the morning of the fifth. The pathologist, a stooped, patient woman named Dr. Mitra with a heavy cardigan under her white coat, went over him slowly and in silence for an hour, then came out to the corridor to find Sen sitting on a plastic chair with his head against the wall.
"He was in pain," she said. "Not at the end. For months. The lymphoma had gone into the nodes behind his knees and up into the liver. He must have been walking on broken glass every day." She looked at the file in her hands. "And he kept a schedule. Did you see the calendar in the kitchen? Every day for a year, he marked off the hour he took his tablets, and the hour he tested his hands for tremor, and the hour he wound the kitchen clock. He was counting. Right to the end."
"Counting what?"
Dr. Mitra looked at him for a moment, then shrugged, a small, tired movement.
"I think," she said, "he was counting how long it would take the rest of us to understand him."
---
Sen went back to the lab in Salt Lake that afternoon, to the room with the window and the goat.
Dr. Paul was already there. She had laid out the evidence on the long table in neat rows: the collar, the two kitchen timers, the cracked lathe bit, the box of tool-steel offcuts, a plastic bag of Raniganj coal dust, the first and second pages of the hand-drawn map, a strip of brown tape, a jar of dusty glass with a red wax seal.
"He used the same mould for everything," she said, pointing. "The jar from the Grand Trunk Road milestone, the jar from the flowerbed. The tape from the flowerbed in Park Street, the card from the Garden Reach shop. The timers from his desk. The lathe bits from the Asansol shop." She picked up a plastic bag and held it to the light. "It's all the same man's handwriting, Inspector. Just in different materials."
"Was there anything in the jars?"
"The milestone jar had nothing in it but dust. Whoever sealed it wiped the glass first." She set the bag down. "The Park Street flowerbed had a torn sheet of paper that said Act now, think later, or you will die. We couldn't match it to anything."
"No. I don't suppose we could."
Dr. Paul studied him. Then she looked away, out the window, at the wild grass and the cleaner sweeping the path.
"You've never worked a case like this, have you?" she said.
"No."
"Nobody has." She picked up the lathe bit and turned it slowly in her fingers, examining the chipped edge. "It's a puzzle box, Inspector. Every piece fits, and every piece is a lie, and the box doesn't open unless you know which piece was the lie in the first place. He didn't leave you a crime. He left you an argument that you can never win."
Sen did not reply.
He picked up the first page of the map and studied the hand-drawn streets, the careful lettering, the small red square marked on the edge of the green patch. He thought of a man in a grey raincoat standing in a lot full of police cars, watching a bank manager's cardigan flutter in the wind. He thought of a clerk named Ghoshal, with her hair in a bun and her fingers grey with coal dust, counting out eight thousand seven hundred and twenty rupees with her eyes on the bundles and not on the man.
He thought of a plastic cup of coffee on the morning of the twelfth of May. A text from an unknown number. Two small pizzas. Ask for the gate.
And he thought of a line of dead marigolds on a bank's top step, left there by somebody on a festival day nobody remembered.

Chapter 14: The Last Laugh
The newspapers ran the story for six weeks, then lost interest.
The Asansol Bulletin printed the widow's photograph on its front page under the headline WIDOW SURVIVES AS CHAIRMAN'S HOUSE FALLS, and then reprinted the same photograph a fortnight later, cropped, under a different headline about a state committee reviewing municipal zoning. The Kolkata Chronicle ran three long articles about the Hooghly Commercial Bank robbery, each more breathless than the last, each with a diagram of the collar that bore only a passing resemblance to the real thing. A television channel in Delhi flew a team to the Garden Reach shop and filmed the front window for two minutes while a presenter stood in the lane and spoke in a low, grave voice about the dangers of mechanical obsession.
Nobody filmed the freezer. Nobody filmed the library's back wall, which was propped up with scaffolding and a row of rusting oil drums, or the hollow concrete basement of Farooq Hardware Supplies, which had been filled in by the municipality with three truckloads of sand and never mentioned again.
The Komatsu D355A was lifted out of the Asansol pit by a crane from Panagarh on the eleventh of June, two days after the army had cut through the hatch. Inside, the cab smelled of burnt oil and something older, and the fold-down table was clean. The paperweight had been removed. The list, a single ruled sheet in square, deliberate writing, was never found.
The cassette recorder was never found either.
The brother in Kharagpur was interviewed by a constable in a tea shop by the railway station. He sat with his hands folded on the table and his eyes on the steam rising from his cup, and when the constable asked whether he had listened to his brother's tape, he said, after a long pause, "Only the first ten minutes."
"And?"
"And he was right about the thing he was angry about," the brother said. "He was always right about the thing he was angry about. That was never the problem."
---
Madhumita Ghosh pleaded guilty but mentally ill to the murder of Jayanta Roy in a courtroom on Esplanade in the autumn of 2005. Her lawyer, a man in a crumpled grey suit with a face like a folded handkerchief, argued for four hours that his client's bipolar disorder had driven her to act without reason. The judge listened without visible expression. When he delivered his sentence, he did not look at her once.
Her conviction for conspiracy to commit bank robbery came in 2010, in a special court in Delhi, after a long and bitter trial in which the prosecution produced a witness who had sat across from her in a car outside the Doordarshan mast on the marsh road and heard her laugh. The laugh had gone on for almost a minute, the witness said. It was the only time in his life he had been afraid of a person's voice.
Kamal "Kenny" Haldar had been the first to break. He sat in an interrogation room at Lalbazar with his hands shaking on the table, sweating in his grease-stained mechanic's cap, a fishing lure dangling from one vest pocket, and he confessed to everything, including several things that had never happened. The judge gave him a lighter sentence in exchange for his cooperation. He was released after eight years. He returned to fishing on the Hooghly.
Nobody ever found the woman in the flat above the tea stall on Rashbehari Avenue. The rent on her room had been paid in cash until the end of the year. After that, the room stayed empty.
---
Inspector Arindam Sen retired from the Lalbazar Detective Branch in 2009, after twenty-six years and a commendation he did not want. He took a small flat in a lane in Behala, with a window that looked out over a stand of tamarind trees and a family of mynahs who fought constantly over the crumbs he left on the sill.
He kept the file in a cardboard box under the bed.
It was not an official file. Official files were kept in the basement at Lalbazar, in grey metal cabinets with yellowed labels, and they were never read by anyone except the clerks who filed them. This one was his own. It contained the photographs from the lab, the two-page map in a plastic sleeve, a cheap Dum Dum lollipop, its cellophane torn, that had been found in the pocket of a delivery shirt, and a single printed sheet from the Asansol coroner's office, dated 4 June 2004.
It listed the cause of death for an unidentified male, age forty-eight, found in the cab of an industrial bulldozer. The cause was given as a single gunshot wound to the head. The manner of death was given as self-inflicted, undetermined. The coroner had added a note at the bottom in pencil, in a different hand, possibly the clerk's.
Victim's name withheld pending family notification. Identity confirmed as Pratap Banerjee, Asansol. Family has declined to comment.
Sen read the sheet every few weeks, in the evenings, with a glass of whisky on the table beside him and the mynahs squabbling outside in the tamarind leaves. He did not know why he kept reading it. He knew the answer already. The welder had not been the mastermind. He had been the lathe.
The mastermind had been a dying man in a garage in Garden Reach, with a bottle of cough syrup and a photograph of his wife, and a shelf of finished work that nobody could ever fully explain. He had given the welder his grievance and his list. He had given the woman her hitman and her grudge. He had given the bank manager a cardigan to fold and a coal-dusted strapping to count, and the police a bag of dead marigolds to chase through the sun. He had built a puzzle that asked a single question, and the question was not who.
The question was why would a man spend his last months making the world wait for him?
And Sen, sitting in the failing light with the glass sweating in his hand, had come to suspect that the answer was not in any of the jars.
It was in the note on the desk. This has nothing to do with the Hooghly Commercial Bank case. The sentence was true. It had been written by a man who never told a lie he could not make true later. And every piece of evidence in the box under Sen's bed supported that sentence, and every single one of them pointed, with the patient, mechanical certainty of a clock hand, toward a bank in Dalhousie Square that had been robbed of eight thousand seven hundred and twenty rupees and a Dum Dum lollipop.
Sen sometimes wondered if that was the joke.
He had never been able to decide.
---
Once a year, on the anniversary of the tide table, a single envelope arrived at Sen's flat in Behala. It came by post, with no return address, and the postmark was always from a different district of the city. Inside was always the same thing: a torn page from a Bengali panjika, the thick almanac every family kept beside the Ganesh calendar, with the new moon circled in red and a single time printed beneath it.
High water: 03:14.
Sen never went to Kidderpore to check.
He put the page in the box with the rest of the file, and he poured himself a glass of whisky, and he watched the mynahs fight over the crumbs until the light went out of the tamarind leaves and the lane fell silent under the first monsoon rain.
The rain came down steadily, soft and grey, the way it had come down on the night the boy in the red vest first saw the bank manager's cardigan flutter in the wind.
Sen did not turn on the lamp.
He sat in the dark and listened to the water running off the roof and into the drain, down toward the river, toward the place where the tide came up to the mark on the pillar at 3:14, and where, somewhere under the grey surface, a small glass jar with a red wax seal sat in the mud, empty, and waiting for someone to ask what the river was for.
END OF BENGAL NOIR
