PART ONE: KOLKATA
The Ticking Clock
---
Chapter 1: Two Small Pizzas for a Dead Tower
The oven at Napoli Pizza on Gariahat Road had been running since eight, and by half past one it had turned the back kitchen into a tin box that smelled of scorched cheese and wet cardboard. Bijoy Das stood at the prep counter with his shirt stuck to his spine and his fingers greasy with oregano, folding boxes the way his mother had folded letters, corner, corner, tuck, so nothing could get in.
Forty-six years old. Nine years at Napoli. Nobody at the counter had ever once asked him to speak up.
The phone in his back pocket buzzed against the plastic chair. He did not take it out. He knew the buzz. It was the short, nervous pulse Kenny Haldar sent when he had something to say that he did not want to say in writing.
Dada. Thursday. Be ready.
Bijoy folded another box.
He had owed the money for fourteen months. It had started with a small thing, a bit of white powder bought for a night when he could not sleep, a night after his sister stopped answering the phone. It had grown, the way water grows in a basement, quietly, until one day it was on every floor. The woman in the flat above the tea stall on Rashbehari Avenue knew his name. Kenny knew his address. Kenny also knew that Bijoy liked animals more than he liked people, and that a man who liked animals was a man who could be reminded of his responsibilities.
At home there were three cats. Lakhi, who was grey and bad-tempered. Bhutu, who was small and bit everything. And Mantri, a fat orange tom who had come in through the window one monsoon and decided he would stay. Bijoy had not chosen a single one of them. He had simply never managed to send any of them out.
"Das-da, the Doordarshan order." Biswajit, the manager, leaned in from the front with his headset pushed up into his hair. "Two small pizzas. Cheese and tomato, one with no onions, one with extra. It's pre-paid. Cash on delivery, but the caller says it's pre-paid, so don't argue with the caller."
"Where?"
"The relay mast. Past Dhapa, on the marsh road." Biswajit read it off the screen with his mouth turned down. "Who orders pizza to a mast?"
"Maybe the engineers."
"Engineers eat rice."
Bijoy took the order slip. The address was typed, neat and small. Below it, someone had written in pen, in a careful round hand: Ask for the gate. Don't wait at the road.
"Thirty minutes," Biswajit said. "Guaranteed. If you're late, the boss takes it from your pay, so don't be late."
"I'm never late."
"I know." Biswajit almost smiled. "That's why I'm sending you past Dhapa."
---
The scooter was a Hero with a cracked seat and a rear light that flickered like a dying match. He strapped the two boxes into the insulated bag with a bungee cord, checked the mirrors, and pushed off into the traffic on Gariahat Road with his helmet strap loose and the afternoon heat pressing down like a hand.
Kolkata in the monsoon's tail end was a city that did not dry out. The air hung heavy with the breath of a million cooking fires and the sweet rot of mango skins in the gutters. Trams ground along the tracks on Rashbehari, yellow and tired, their bells clanging at the buses. A hand-pulled rickshaw wobbled past with its driver's shirt stuck to his back. Bijoy ducked between a bread van and a black Ambassador with its horn stuck on, and he thought, not for the first time, that the city did not notice him any more than it noticed the potholes.
He liked the ride. It was the only part of the job that made him feel like a man moving through his own life rather than hiding in a back room.
Past the Ballygunge bridge the buildings shrank. The brick gave way to tin and then to open ground, low marshes, the glint of fish ponds, the sudden stink of the Dhapa dump on the wind, smoke from burning rubbish and something wetter underneath. Crows walked along the edges of the road like men on their way to work. A goat stood in a puddle with its eyes shut.
The marsh road ran straight for three kilometres with no shade at all. Then the relay mast came up out of the haze.
It was enormous, a lattice of steel legs and dish arms that stood in the middle of nothing like a rusted skeleton waiting for someone to tell it the joke. Around its base a chain-link fence sagged under creepers. A concrete hut squatted at the foot of the mast with its windows painted over. A single bulb burned above a steel gate, though it was broad daylight.
Bijoy stopped the scooter on the shoulder and checked the time. One fifty-two. He had eight minutes on the clock and nothing but cheese in his bag.
He pushed the scooter to the gate. The chain was not locked. A man stood in the shade of the hut's doorway, half in and half out, a dark shape with his face turned toward the road.
He was tall. He wore a canvas work coat that was too heavy for the day, the sleeves rolled to the elbow, and he had a thick grey moustache trimmed with unusual care. His hands were large and clean. He held one of them up in a small, courteous gesture, as if greeting a guest at a dinner party.
"Dada," he said. "Ask for the gate, you asked. Good. Most people wait at the road."
"Two pizzas," Bijoy said. His voice came out smaller than he meant.
"I know. Bring them in, please. It's hot out here."
Bijoy stepped through the gate. The man did not reach for the bag. He stood aside to let him pass, and Bijoy saw that behind him, in the dim room of the hut, a second man was already waiting, leaning against a steel workbench. He was younger, with a shaved head and a nervous, restless stillness, the stillness of a man who had been told to wait and was not used to it.
And on the workbench, in a neat row, lay a thing made of iron.
It was a collar. A heavy hinged ring of dark metal, wide enough to close around a man's neck, with three steel bands clamped to its outside and four keyholes set into the hinge. Two cylindrical casings were bolted to its back. Wires ran from the casings to a cluster of cheap plastic devices: a pair of round red kitchen timers, the kind that sat on the second shelf of every household in the city, and a black box with a digital display showing a number that did not yet mean anything.
The number was fifteen minutes.
Bijoy stared at it. The pizza boxes slid in the bag on his shoulder.
"Put the bag down, Dada," the tall man said pleasantly. "You're going to need your hands."

Chapter 2: A Collar for a Delivery Boy
He tried to run, and that was the first mistake, because the younger man was faster than he looked and did not have to think about it.
Bijoy got three steps back toward the gate before a forearm hit his chest and drove him against the steel bench. The pizza bag fell. A box split open. The smell of cheese and tomato filled the hut, absurd and warm. Hands pinned his wrists. The younger man's breath was sour with cigarettes and something sweeter underneath, a tang of chemical fizz.
"Stop moving, Dada," the younger man said quietly. "Stop, or the thing goes off now, not later. Nobody wants that."
"Please," Bijoy said. He did not recognise his own voice. "Please, I have cats."
"Cats," the tall man said, almost fondly, as if the word had given him a small pleasure. "Good. Tell me their names. Tell me while my friend holds you."
"Lakhi. Bhutu. Mantri."
"Mantri. That's a good cat name. You chose it?"
"No. He came in through the window."
"Ah." The tall man nodded slowly, appreciating this. "Then he chose you. That's a lot more than most men get."
He came round the bench. Bijoy could see him now without the glare of the road behind him: a long, weathered face with a high forehead and very pale grey eyes, steady as glass. Those eyes did not blink much. They travelled over Bijoy's face the way a man checks a measurement, and then down to his neck.
"Hold him still, Kenny."
"I know how to hold him, Bimu-da."
"Then hold him better."
The tall man brought a flexible steel measuring tape from his coat and wrapped it once around Bijoy's throat, pulled it snug, read the number at the buckle, and wrote it on the back of the pizza slip with a stub of pencil. Bijoy could smell machine oil on the tape. It was cold against his skin, and then the cold was gone and his neck remembered it.
"Forty-two centimetres, with allowance," the tall man murmured. "Madhumita will be pleased. She said you were a small man, but she didn't say how much room you'd leave."
He lifted the iron collar from the bench. It was heavier than it looked, a dense bitter weight, and when the younger man released Bijoy's wrists and pushed him down onto a stool, the tall man stepped behind him and fitted it into place with a delicacy that was almost tender. The hinge closed with a click. Three bands tightened, one under his jaw, one across the top of his skull, one at the base of his throat. The casings settled against his spine, cold and round, and the wires ran down his chest like cold roots.
"Don't touch it," the tall man said. "Don't pull. The pipes sit on your spine. If you lean back, they lean with you."
Bijoy's breath came out in a sob he could not stop. The collar bit his jaw. The timers ticked, two of them out of step with each other, one a little ahead.
"Who are you?"
"My name is Bimal Chakraborty. People in Garden Reach know me as the man who fixes their washing machines and doesn't charge for the second visit. You may call me what they call me."
"Bimu."
"Mm. Small things, Bijoy. Better than big ones." He stepped round to face him, and in his hand was a black plastic box no larger than a cigarette packet, with a red switch on its top and a fine grille at its front. He lifted it to his lips, and when he spoke the grille carried his voice in a crackling pulse.
"The deadline is fifteen minutes, Bijoy. Hooghly Commercial Bank, Dalhousie Square, the front counter. You will hand the teller a note. The note will ask for two lakh fifty thousand rupees in five-hundred notes, unmarked, with no bands. The teller will give you what she has. It will be less than that. It will be enough."
"And if it isn't?"
"Then the timers will finish. Kenny, show him the umbrella."
The younger man lifted a long black umbrella from a hook beside the door. It was an ordinary thing, a gentleman's cane umbrella with a curved wooden handle worn pale by years of hands. He turned it over, unscrewed the handle with a twist, and drew out a length of blued steel that caught the bulb's light. The barrel was rough-machined. There was a crude trigger guard welded to the underside of the shaft.
"Two feet," Kenny said proudly. "Single shot. Holds the bank guard's attention very well."
"It's a gun," Bijoy whispered.
"It's an umbrella," Bimu corrected. "Today, it's an umbrella with a gun inside it. Tomorrow it's back in the shop. Nobody will look at a man carrying an umbrella in the rain."
"It's not raining."
"Then they'll think you're prepared." He smiled, the smile of a man who enjoyed being clever. "Now the shirt."
From a plastic bag on the bench he took out a crisp white T-shirt, bought, Bijoy thought, from a wholesale stall in Burrabazar that sold cheap copies of foreign brands. The word on the front was printed in a bold, sporty script: GUEST. Bimu pulled it over Bijoy's head like a child's clothes, over his delivery shirt and his worn blue polo, tugged the hem straight, and stepped back to admire it.
"There," he said. "Now they'll be sure to notice you."
Bijoy looked down at the iron around his throat and the cheap letters on his chest and the number on the black box, eleven minutes now, ticking, and he understood that nothing in his life had prepared him for the sheer calm in the man's voice.
"Why me?"
Bimu looked at him for a long moment. Something almost like pity moved across the glass of his eyes and was gone.
"Because you're alone, Bijoy," he said. "And because everyone who matters to you has already forgotten your name."
From the rear of the hut came the sound of a car engine, low and patient. The younger man was already at the door. Bimu lifted the umbrella and placed it in Bijoy's hands, closing his fingers around the curved handle.
"Go on," the voice in the box said, very softly. A woman's voice, somewhere far away, a voice that had been listening the whole time. "Dalhousie. Your mother's waiting up."
Bijoy had no mother living. The woman knew that. He understood, in the moment before the car door slammed behind him, that the woman had not made a mistake.

Chapter 3: Dum Dum, Please
The bank was a pale yellow colonial box at the corner of Dalhousie Square, with a portico of tired columns and a name carved above the door in letters that had once been gold. Hooghly Commercial Bank. Est. 1921. A bunch of dead marigolds lay on the top step, left there by a clerk for some festival nobody remembered.
Bijoy walked up the steps with the umbrella in one hand and the note folded in the other. The collar pulled at his jaw. Every step sent the casings knocking against his spine like a fist on a door. The timers ticked in his ears, and under them, the sound of his own breath, too fast.
He thought about Mantri, the orange tom, sitting on the windowsill above the Gariahat lane, waiting for the sound of the scooter. He thought about the empty bag on the bench in the hut. He thought the cat would be angry about the pizza.
The bank's doors were glass, reflecting the square. In the glass he saw a thin man in a white T-shirt with the word GUEST across his chest and a black umbrella hung over his forearm like a waiter's towel. The man's face was grey. The man looked, Bijoy thought, like someone waiting for a bus that would never come.
He pushed through the doors.
The air inside was cold. Air conditioning rattled in the ceiling vents, and the floor was a shine of polished marble, and the smell was floor wax and cigarettes and the faint dry breath of paper. Four people stood in the queue at the counter. A child was crying in a pram somewhere behind a pillar. Two guards in khaki shirts stood by the revolving door, one of them eating a chikki from a paper packet, the sugar crumbling down his chin.
Bijoy walked to the front of the queue. Nobody stopped him. Nobody quite looked at him. The clerk at the first window was a young woman with her hair in a tight bun and a nameplate that read S. Ghoshal, and she glanced up with the bored, polite irritation of someone who had been sitting in the same chair since eight in the morning.
"Yes?"
Bijoy put the note on the counter.
The clerk read it. Her eyes moved across the typed lines. Her mouth slowly closed.
He had seen it on the way in, read it in the hut, and it had been typed in the same neat hand as the address on the pizza slip. Two lakh fifty thousand. Five-hundreds. No bands. Do not press the alarm. Do not speak to the police. Your colleague is watching the back door.
"Sir," the clerk said, very quietly, "this is not a joke."
"No," Bijoy said.
"Sir, you have to step back from the counter."
"I can't." His voice shook. "The collar."
The clerk's gaze dropped. It slid over the iron ring round his neck, over the thick wires, the casings beneath the white shirt, and then it lifted again to his face, and her own face went white in a single movement, like a sheet being pulled off a bed.
"Gopal-da," she whispered, not taking her eyes off him. "Gopal-da, call the manager. Now. Don't run."
Behind her, a supervisor with thick glasses dropped the phone he was holding. A man in the queue made a noise and sat down on the floor. The guard with the chikki stopped chewing.
Bijoy lifted the umbrella. He did not know why, except that it was the thing the tall man had told him to do with his hands, and his hands had nothing else to do. He pointed the curved handle at the guard by the revolving door.
"Don't," he said.
The guard looked at the umbrella. He looked at the GUEST shirt, the grey face, the shaking wrist. He put the chikki down on the marble with great care and raised both hands.
Behind the counter the clerk had begun to cry, silently, her lips pressed together, her hands flat on the wood. She did not stop the cash drawer from opening. She did not stop anything. She simply slid it out, and inside it were bundles of notes wrapped in paper bands, and the paper bands had a grey dust on them that Bijoy could see from where he stood, like ash on a coal fire.
"Sir," the clerk said through her tears, "I can give you eight thousand seven hundred and twenty. That's all the front counter has. The rest is in the vault. The vault needs the manager. Please, the manager is coming."
"Eight thousand seven hundred and twenty."
"Yes."
"Count it out."
She counted it. Her fingers were quick, even through the shaking. She placed the notes in a plastic bag and pushed the bag across the counter. She did not look up while she did it. She looked at the bag, and at the bundles, and at the grey dust that clung to her fingertips afterwards.
Bijoy took the bag. Under the counter, in a small glass dish, sat a single lollipop, red and white, unwrapped and half-licked. He stared at it. It was a Dum Dum lollipop, the cheap sugar-stick kind sold in every paan shop from here to Howrah. Somebody had left it there. Somebody, perhaps, who had been sitting at this counter all day.
He reached out, took it, and put it in his pocket. He did not know why. It was the only thing in the room that did not belong to the bank.
The voice came out of the box on his chest, crisp and amused.
"Lollipops are for children, Bijoy. You'll want to remember that."
The timer read nine.
He backed away from the counter with the bag over one shoulder and the umbrella in his fist. The clerk did not look up. The child behind the pillar had stopped crying and was watching him with enormous eyes. The revolving door turned slowly under his push, and then he was outside, in the heat, in the hard white light of two-forty in the afternoon, and every person on the square turned their head at once.
He ran.
---
Chapter 4: Act Now, Think Later
He made it three hundred metres before the first police jeep came round the Writers' Building with its siren off and its light going round, a dull red pulse against the yellow walls.
Bijoy cut south into the lanes. The collar bounced against his collarbone. The bag of notes slapped his thigh with every step, heavy and stupid. People stepped out of his way without quite meeting his eyes. A tea seller shouted. A tram rang its bell at him from Lal Dighi, and he flung himself sideways into a doorway, the umbrella clattering on the stone, and a stout woman with a basket of bananas looked down at him with pure contempt.
"Ki holo, ki? Mad?"
He picked up the umbrella and ran again.
At Park Street the crowds thickened. Tourists in shorts. Schoolboys in grey trousers. A fat man selling roasted corn from a drum. Neon signs stuttered on the wet pavements. Somewhere a speaker blared a Hindi film song, bright and tinny, the chorus spilling over the traffic. Bijoy ducked under the awning of a fast-food restaurant with a plastic cow painted on its window and stopped, bent double, his breath tearing in and out of his chest.
On the pavement beside the restaurant's drive-through lane sat a flowerbed. It was a raised square of cracked concrete filled with dusty marigolds and a single patch of thin grass, and in the middle of it, half-buried in the dirt, lay a grey stone the size of a fist.
Taped to the stone with two strips of brown tape was a folded slip of paper.
Bijoy looked at it. He looked at the timer on his chest. Six minutes.
He crouched, pulled the stone aside, peeled the tape. Inside the fold was a single line, typed in the same clean hand.
Act now, think later, or you will die.
He stared at it. The letters blurred, then cleared. He thought of the clerk's white face, the grey dust on her fingers, the crying child behind the pillar. He thought of the sound of his own name being spoken by a voice in a box.
"Dada, you're making a scene," said the voice. It was almost fond. "Go on. Read the second part. It's on the back of the map."
He turned the slip over. It was not a slip at all. It was a folded sheet of paper, thick and soft, two pages of it, covered in careful hand-drawn lines. Streets. Crossroads. The long ruled curve of the Grand Trunk Road. A small square marked in red ink on the edge of a green patch, labelled in Bengali and then in English, the lettering so neat it seemed engraved. Beside the square, a note.
Orange tape. Glass. Do not stop to think.
A police siren wailed close behind him, two streets over. Bijoy stood up, folded the map into his shirt pocket with the lollipop, and began to run again, north to south, toward a road he did not know, with the iron pulling at his jaw.
Three minutes later he was on the Grand Trunk Road, the lorries roaring past in a wall of noise and diesel smoke, and a dusty bus had stopped at the kerb to let a goat cross. He looked at the map again. He looked at the land beyond the highway, where the fields ran flat and brown toward a line of dark trees.
On the turning he found a wooden sign nailed to a post. Orange tape, looped twice around the base of the post and knotted tight.
Beyond it, at the edge of the thicket, half-hidden under a sprawl of dry weeds, sat a cheap plastic container with its lid taped shut and a flash of orange on its side.
He ran for it, the umbrella banging against his leg. Behind him, the sirens turned the corner onto the highway, and a dark police van came up the road like a shark through brown water.

Chapter 5: The Empty Jar
The container was the size of a biscuit tin, and when Bijoy tore off the tape with shaking fingers, he found inside a glass jar, clean and sealed, its metal lid stamped with a faded brand. He unscrewed the lid, lifted the jar free, and held it up to the thin sunlight that came through the trees.
It was empty.
Not a scrap of paper, not a coin, not a thread of cloth. Just glass, and a faint film of dust on the inside wall, and the slow white swirl of his own breath condensing on the rim.
He turned the jar over. Nothing underneath. He shook it. The sound was thin and hollow, the sound of a thing that had been meant to hold something and had never been given the chance.
"Dada," said the box, "you're looking for something that isn't there."
Bijoy's knees gave, and he sat down in the dust with the jar in his lap.
"It's a test," the voice went on, gently, reasonably. "It's always a test, Bijoy. The men in the vans are listening, and so are the men in the tower. So you'll understand something now that you'll be grateful for later. Nobody was ever going to find the thing at the end. The thing at the end was never there. The clock was there. The notes were there. The rest was just to keep the police running."
"You said there was something."
"I said there was a game. You believed me. That's the only part I'm sorry about." A pause, a soft exhale of breath through the grille. "The timers are at four minutes."
Bijoy looked down at his chest. The two red kitchen timers ticked side by side, their thin hands creeping toward the top of their dials. The black digital box above them blinked four, four, four.
From the road came the sound of the van's engine, idling, then cutting out. Doors opened. Boots hit the dirt.
"Sit still," the voice said, and for the first time there was no amusement in it at all. "Sit very still. Put the jar down. You're not going to be able to run anymore."
He put the jar down.
They came through the trees with their guns up. Eight of them, in khaki and dust, one with a long rifle and a face he recognised from somewhere: the tallest, the one in the grey raincoat, who had been waiting at the Doordarshan mast in his head all afternoon without ever having been there. He did not shout. He stopped twenty metres from Bijoy and held one hand out, palm down, very slowly, the way a man calms a dog.
"Bijoy Das," he said. "Inspector Arindam Sen, Lalbazar Detective Branch. You are going to put your hands on your head, and then you are going to lie down on the ground. Nobody else is going to die here today. I promise you."
"I can't lie down," Bijoy whispered. "The collar."
"I know about the collar. We've known about it since the bank." Sen took another step, slow and even. "Tell me what the timer says."
"Four."
"Four minutes." Sen's jaw tightened. "All right. Four minutes is enough for a conversation. Let's have one. Who gave you the bag?"
Bijoy opened his mouth. Nothing came out.
From the box on his chest, the voice spoke again. Quiet. Pleasant.
"Tell the Inspector, Bijoy. You don't owe anyone anything anymore."
Bijoy looked at the iron around his throat, at the number ticking down. He thought of Mantri on the windowsill, and of Kenny Haldar's soft, nervous text. He thought, for no reason he could explain, of the lollipop in his shirt pocket, sweet and sticky, melting against the folds of the map.
"I don't know her name," he said. "I only know she likes timetables."
Sen's face did not change. But something behind his eyes went very still.
"Then you'll tell me the rest in the van," he said. "Down. Now. Hands behind your back."
Bijoy knelt in the dust. He put his hands behind him. The metal cuffs bit into his wrists, cold and final. Two officers lifted him by the armpits and walked him back toward the road, and his legs were jelly under him, and the umbrella lay forgotten in the weeds with its wooden handle pointing at the sky.
Behind him, the jar sat in the dust, empty and clean, and the thin white light came through the trees and lay across it like a line of chalk.
---
Chapter 6: Asphalt, Four Minutes, Twenty-Five
They did not take him to the van. The bomb squad was forty minutes out from Lalbazar, and the sergeant in charge would not move an injured man into an unshielded vehicle without a sandbag rig, and so Bijoy Das was laid down on the hot black asphalt of a parking lot behind a supermarket on the Grand Trunk Road, in a line of cruisers, with his hands cuffed behind him and his cheek pressed against the tar.
It was two forty-three in the afternoon. The timers ticked. The collar hummed.
He could see the lot from where he lay. A wall of white cars, their roofs glinting. A cow chewing cardboard by a dumpster. A small boy in a red vest pressed against the window of a bakery, watching the police with delighted horror, his nose squashed flat against the glass. Beyond the boy, the thick green line of the trees around the highway. The air smelled of diesel and burning plastic and, faintly, of the sugar syrup from the bakery's ovens.
A ring of officers stood at a distance with their weapons trained on him. Between them and Bijoy stood a sandbag barrier, hastily piled, and Inspector Sen crouched behind it with a radio in one hand and a cigarette in the other.
He had not lit the cigarette. He kept turning it in his fingers.
"Bijoy," he said. "Can you hear me?"
"Yes."
"Good. Keep your head still. The sergeant's watching you. He doesn't like you."
"I'm not doing anything."
"I know. That's what worries him."
The timer on his chest read one hundred and eighteen seconds. He could hear every tick.
A van with a news logo rolled to the far end of the lot and stopped behind a row of parked scooters. A man with a telephoto lens climbed out, slung the camera onto his shoulder, and began shooting before anyone could tell him not to. A reporter in a pink blouse took a microphone from her colleague and began to speak to the camera in a low, urgent voice, her eyes wide, her hand pressed flat against her chest.
Bijoy watched the lens swing toward him. He saw his own face on the little screen of the monitor behind the camera. The GUEST shirt was streaked with dust and grease. The collar was dark against his throat. His eyes looked huge and terrified and very old.
"It's going to go off," he said, to nobody. "I'm not lying."
"Nobody thinks you're lying, Bijoy," said Sen from behind the sandbags.
"I'm not lying." His voice cracked. "It's going to go off. I told them I'd lose the shop. I told them about the cats."
"Who did you tell?"
"Kenny. The woman. The old man at the bank." Bijoy tried to lift his head, failed, and let it drop against the tar. "Can you call my boss? Napoli. Biswajit. Tell him I'm sorry about the boxes. Tell him the pizza's on me."
Something moved in the crowd of officers. A young constable put one hand over his mouth and turned his face away.
The timer beeped. It was not a loud sound. It was a small, quick chirp, like a bird landing on a wire.
Bijoy shifted his knees under him, trying to find a way to kneel, and the casings rolled against his spine and the wires drew tight across his chest, and he felt his legs begin to scrape backward over the asphalt, an inch, then two, the way a crab moves, without thought, without any plan except the animal need to be somewhere else.
"Stop moving," said the sergeant.
"I'm trying to get away from it."
"You won't get away from it that way."
Bijoy stopped. He lay still. Sweat ran into his eyes and stung. He could hear the timer. He could hear the news reporter's voice rising, the camera humming, the boy in the red vest crying out for his mother. He could hear, very faintly, a woman's laugh coming out of the grille at his throat. A short, clipped sound, almost bored.
"Dada, you're doing wonderfully," the voice said. "Keep going."
The beeping quickened. It came faster now, a rising pulse, a bird flapping off a wire and onto another wire, and then another. The officers flinched. The sergeant began to swear in a flat, steady voice under his breath. Sen stood up behind the sandbags, flicked the cigarette away, and walked forward a single step.
"Bijoy," he said. "Look at me."
Bijoy lifted his face off the tar and looked. Sen's eyes were grey and steady and very tired.
"Thirty seconds," Sen said. "Tell me one true thing before it goes."
"I didn't want to do it."
"I believe you."
"I wanted a cat. A proper one. Not from the window."
Sen nodded slowly. "I believe you about that too."
The timer beeped once more, and then the sound stopped.
For a single long moment there was nothing in the lot but silence, a silence so complete that Bijoy could hear the boy's sobbing and the slow tick of the news camera's motor and the faint hum of the highway a kilometre away. The collar was still tight around his neck. The number in the black box read zero, and then it read zero again, and nothing happened.
Then the bomb squad van came round the corner of the supermarket with its siren screaming and its tyres throwing up black smoke, and the sergeant shouted, and every officer in the lot turned to look.
Bijoy Das was still looking at Sen when the first pipe charge went off.
It did not take long. Three minutes before the van arrived, the collar tore open along its hinge with a flash of white and a sound like the world being struck by a bell, and the steel bands sprang outward, and the casing exploded in a spray of hot iron and plastic that cut across the asphalt and through the sandbags and into the cruiser doors. The bakery window shattered. The boy in the red vest fell flat on the pavement and did not get up.
Sen was thrown backward over the sandbag wall. He lay on his back on the tar with his arms out, his face streaked with black, looking up at a sky that was perfectly blue.
He did not move for a long time.
Then he did, slowly, pushing himself up on one elbow. He looked across the lot to the place where Bijoy Das had been lying. There was nothing left there but a dark, spreading stain on the asphalt, and a scrap of white cloth with the word GUEST stitched across it, stiff and scorched.
Sen picked up a cigarette from the tar. It was crushed and wet. He held it between two fingers and stared at it.
Somewhere behind him, the young constable was saying, over and over, in a high, shaking voice, "Sir, the boy's alive, the boy is alive."
Sen said nothing. He did not look at the boy. He was looking at the wet, broken cigarette, and at the words that the voice in the box had said, very softly, in the moment before the blast.
Your mother's waiting up.
