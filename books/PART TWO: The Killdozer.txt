PART TWO: ASANSOL
The Killdozer

Chapter 7: The Silencer Works
Asansol in June was a city that had forgotten how to breathe.
The heat came up off the ground like something alive. It sat on the roofs of the coal-dust houses and pressed on the windows of the bus stands, and the sal trees along the railway embankment stood in ranks of dying green, their leaves coated grey with soot. The air tasted of iron and sulphur. Every surface carried a fine film of black: the rails, the bicycle handles, the sleeves of the children who sold peanuts at the Station Road crossing. Coal trucks ground up the highway from the mines at Raniganj, their beds heaped and sagging, and the wind off the dumps came over the town in soft grey waves.
On Grand Trunk Road South, past the brick kilns and the foundry sheds, between a row of wholesale hardware stores and a half-finished concrete plant with its silo standing up against the sky like a broken tooth, there was a muffler shop with a sign hand-painted in red.
ASANSOL SILENCER WORKS. P. BANERJEE, PROP. ALL VEHICLES. NO CREDIT.
Pratap Banerjee was forty-eight years old, and he had been a welder for twenty-six of them. He had hands like shovels, scarred across the knuckles in small white shining dots, the burns of a thousand sparks. He had a thick neck, a close-cropped grey head, and a face that people remembered for its stillness, the way a man remembers a closed door. He spoke little. When he did speak, it was in a flat Bengali with the hard edges of the coal belt, and every word came out as if it had been measured first.
He had grievances. He kept them in a shoebox under the cash counter, folded in strict order.
The first was a bill. A customer named Mishra had brought in a Maruti van with a cracked exhaust and refused to pay the one thousand two hundred and forty rupees he owed for the repair. Pratap had walked round the van once, slowly, then crouched beside the front wheel and said, not loudly, that he would find Mishra's wife at the market on Friday, and that the wife would not enjoy the conversation. Mishra had paid by noon, in cash, his hands shaking.
The second was the sewer.
When Pratap set up the shop in 2001, the municipality had quoted him a connection fee for the drainage line that ran beneath Grand Trunk Road. Pratap had read the quote, then gone out to the back yard and dug a hole the size of a coffin. He had dropped an old concrete mixer drum into it, cut the top open, and wired the whole thing to the outflow of his wash bay. It worked. For three years, the water had drained into the drum and seeped away into the dry red soil, and nobody came.
Then the municipal inspector came in May 2003 with a clipboard and a Bengal Public Works ID that he kept swinging on a lanyard. He stood in the wash bay looking at the concrete drum and the rust-coloured soil and the smell, and he wrote out a fine for two thousand five hundred rupees, and he put it on the counter.
Pratap did not argue. He wrote a cheque. He signed it, slowly, his heavy hand moving across the paper like a man writing on a wall. Then, under the signature, in the same block capitals he used for the shop sign, he wrote a single word across the face of the cheque.
COWARDS.
He put the cheque in an envelope, sealed it, and handed it to the inspector with a smile that did not reach his eyes.
The inspector did not come back.
The third grievance was the concrete plant.
Two hundred metres from the muffler shop, on the vacant land beside the highway, the Mondal family had applied to build a batching plant for their concrete works. The permit went through the Asansol Municipal Corporation. Pratap had written to the commissioner. He had written to the state Public Works minister. He had hired a lawyer named Dutta, who took his retainer and filed a writ petition in the Calcutta High Court, a document of forty pages with the word nuisance printed in bold type on eleven of them. The court had heard the case for seven months. In the end, it dismissed it, with costs.
The Mondal family offered a compromise in February 2004. They would pay for a proper municipal sewer line from the muffler shop's wash bay to the main drain, a line worth more than the shop itself. They would drop their objections to the batching plant's noise permit if Pratap dropped his. It was a generous offer, and everyone in the district said so.
Pratap sent the envelope back unopened.
After that, he stopped answering the phone. He stopped going to the bank on Fridays. He stopped taking the Maruti vans, and then the Tata trucks, and then anything at all. He closed the shop's front shutter at five every evening and pulled the bolt across it, and he went round to the back of the building, to the old brick garage with the corrugated roof and the reinforced steel doors, and he unlocked the padlock with a key on a leather cord around his neck.
And he did not come out again until morning.
That was how the neighbours began to say his name in the tea stalls, in the low, careful voices people use for men who have stopped being predictable. Banerjee's gone quiet. He's in the garage again. Doing something with the iron.
Nobody asked what. Nobody wanted to know.
In the dark of the garage, behind the steel doors, Pratap Banerjee was building a machine.

Chapter 8: Eighteen Months in the Dark
The machine had come to him in the spring of 2003, through an auction notice pinned to a lamp post in Burdwan.
FOR SALE. KOMATSU D355A INDUSTRIAL DOZER. 60 TONS. OPERATIONAL. AS-IS. CONTACT BILLING OFFICER, EASTERN COALFIELDS LTD., ASANSOL.
The machine was the colour of old rust under a crust of spilled oil. It had been used for four years in the open-cast pits near Kunustoria, digging coal from a land that had been scraped raw, and then it had been parked behind a locked fence for two years while the company waited for someone to make an offer. Nobody made an offer. The tracks had sunk into the mud. The cab glass was cracked in three places.
Pratap walked around it on the day of the auction with his hands in his pockets, and he did not speak to the billing officer once. He touched the blade, cold and heavy, and he ran his palm along the engine housing, and then he looked up at the cab, a thirteen-foot box of steel perched on a frame of thick black treads.
"How much to tow it?"
The billing officer named a price. Pratap named a smaller one. They settled between them, and the officer gave him a receipt for the tractor, an old cloth-backed ledger sheet with a stamp that had faded to a ghost.
Four trucks came to the garage in July, and two more in August. Pratap did not hire anyone to help him. He did the lifting himself, with a steel chain and a hydraulic jack and a pair of old cast-iron rollers he had bought from a scrap dealer in Burnpur. The tractor went inside through the reinforced doors on a flatbed, and the doors closed behind it, and they did not open again for almost a year.
What happened inside the garage, the neighbours heard only in pieces. The grinding began at seven each morning and ran until dusk. There was the scream of a cutting wheel through steel, a sound that set the dogs in the lane howling. There were the flat, sharp reports of an arc welder, blue-white light flickering under the door gap at night like a lightning storm trapped in a box. There was the hollow boom of hammer blows against plate, slow and regular, as if someone were ringing a bell that did not want to be rung.
The garage's back window was covered with sheets of corrugated iron, nailed down. The front shutter stayed closed. Pratap took his meals in the shop's little kitchen, a plate of rice and dal with a boiled egg, and he slept on a cot in the corner of the workshop with his boots on and an arc lamp burning all night.
He cut the tractor down to its frame. He took the cab off and built it up again, larger, from a sheet of two-inch tool-steel plate that came in on a truck from a supplier in Howrah who never asked what it was for. He welded the new cab into place. He welded a hull of steel around the engine, and another hull around that, and in the narrow space between the two layers he poured concrete, a dense grey slurry from a mixer he had bought used from the Mondal concrete works, the irony of it lost on nobody but Pratap. He poured in courses, letting each one set, tamping it down with a steel bar until the air bubbles rose to the surface and burst. Layer after layer. Eleven courses in all. The inner hull bulged under the weight of it and he welded braces across the bulge, and then he poured again.
By the end of the first winter, the machine was twelve inches thick at the frontal plate.
Through the tool-steel hull he cut five ports, each the size of a man's fist, and into each port he fitted a box of clear acrylic-lexan, three inches thick, bulletproof, sealed with rubber gaskets and bolted from the inside. Behind each lexan panel he mounted a camera. Five cameras: front, left, right, rear, and one at the top of the cab, angled down. The feeds ran through coaxial cable into a monitor on the cab's wall. Air blasters, fed from a compressor bolted to the engine frame, sent blasts of pressurised air across each lens to clear dust.
He installed an air conditioner. It was a second-hand split unit taken from a dismantled bank branch in Durgapur, and he wired it to a generator in the engine well. He put in a bed, a fold-down table, a shelf of tinned food and bottled water. A week's rations, he calculated, carefully, with a pencil.
Then he mounted the weapons.
He did not buy them. He made them, or he made room for them. A .50-calibre rifle he took from an arms dealer in Dhanbad who had owed him for a welding job on a dead man's truck. A .308 semi-automatic from the same source. A .22 for small things. Two handguns, a revolver and a pistol, fitted into fixed mounts on the cab's inner walls, angled to fire through the ports. He wired them to triggers on the control console. He tested each one in the garage, with the doors shut, the sound a flat, enormous slam that rattled the iron sheets off the walls and left his ears ringing for a day.
He did not sleep much after that. He sat in the cab at night with the monitor glowing blue and watched the empty lanes of Asansol through his five lexan eyes, and he listened to the compressor hum, and he thought.
In the spring of 2004, he sealed the entry hatch.
It was a heavy steel plate, hinged at the side, with a wheel lock on the inside. He welded it shut from within, using a long, slender electrode, in one unbroken line that took a full day. When he was finished, the hatch was a seam of raised, blue-black metal that no one could open from outside, and the wheel lock had been broken off and dropped into the engine well. There was no exit now except through the ports, and the ports were lexan, and the lexan was bolted shut.
Pratap sat in the cab and listened to the silence.
He thought of the bank, the water-stained concrete drum in the yard, the cheque, the inspector's face. He thought of the tall, pale-eyed stranger from Kolkata, who had come to the shop one afternoon in March with a case of lathe bearings and a folded map, and who had stood by the lathe for an hour watching Pratap turn a steel bar down to a perfect cylinder, saying nothing at all except, at the end, You're wasted on mufflers, Banerjee-da.
He thought of the list.
It lay on the cab's fold-down table now, under a glass paperweight, a single sheet of ruled paper in his own square, deliberate hand. One hundred and seven names. Municipal officials, trustees, judges, rival businessmen, a journalist from the local daily who had written the word fool about him in print, a priest who had signed the petition against his gambling licence in 1998. Each name was numbered. Some had addresses. Some had a single word beside them.
Mondal, Siddharth. Batching plant. Yes.
Lahiri, Mrs. Anima. Widow of former chairman. House on Station Road. She knows what he did.
Farooq Hardware. Owner made the sewer pipe that failed. Yes.
Asansol Municipal Corporation. Commissioner's office. The library. Children.
He had not yet decided about the children.
He had recorded a cassette, too, in the early summer, on a cheap tape recorder perched on a stack of tyre irons. Two and a half hours of his own voice, low and certain, explaining himself. The men who had cheated him. The God who had watched him suffer. The sign that had come when the tractor sold for nothing and nobody noticed the garage. He had mailed the cassette to his brother in Kharagpur, wrapped in brown paper, with a note in pencil: I will sacrifice what I have to show you what you did was wrong.
His brother had listened to the first ten minutes and put the tape in a drawer.
Pratap did not know that. He did not need to know it. He had already decided on June the fourth, because the number four was the number of the Mondal concrete trucks that came through the highway gate every morning, and four was the number of days he had waited for the monsoon to break, and because the sky over Asansol that morning, for the first time in nine weeks, was a flat, shining white with no cloud anywhere in it at all.
He climbed into the cab. He settled into the seat. He put his hands on the controls, the steel levers worn smooth by his own palms over the long months.
Then he turned the key, and the engine of the Komatsu D355A came awake with a roar that set every loose sheet of iron in the garage ringing like a bell.

Chapter 9: The Breach at Two-Oh-Five
At 2:05 in the afternoon on the fourth of June, the Mondal concrete plant's loading foreman, a stocky man named Ramlal, was sitting on an upturned drum beside the batching silo, eating his lunch of puffed rice with onions and green chillies out of a steel tiffin. He was watching the highway. A line of concrete trucks idled at the gate, drivers leaning out of their cabs, smoking, shouting about the heat. He saw the garage doors of the muffler shop move.
He saw them bulge.
He saw them buckle outward, slowly, the corrugated iron groaning and tearing free of its brackets, and then the whole front wall of the brick garage split open with a sound like a freight train snapping its coupling. Dust exploded out in a grey wave. A blade came through the gap, shining, followed by a second blade, then the hull of the machine itself, rust-coloured under its crust of concrete and steel plate, with five small square eyes gleaming in its face.
Ramlal dropped his tiffin on the ground. Rice and onions scattered in the dust.
The Komatsu D355A came through the wall the way a fist comes through paper. It rolled onto the highway with a slow, grinding roar, its tracks crushing the asphalt into powder, and it turned its blade toward the batching plant with the patient deliberation of a tide turning on the beach.
Siddharth Mondal ran out of the plant office in his shirtsleeves with a phone in one hand and a clipboard in the other. He saw the machine and stopped dead. He stood on the loading apron with the phone at his ear and his mouth open, and then he shouted something at the foreman, and the foreman shouted back, and a loader driver jumped down from the seat of a front-end loader parked by the silo and came running with a fist raised and his face contorted with rage.
The loader was a yellow thing, big-wheeled, with a scoop the size of a bathtub. The driver climbed back into it and turned it toward the highway. He gunned the engine. The loader lurched forward across the apron and rammed its bucket into the flank of the Komatsu with a crash that rang across the plant like a church bell.
The Komatsu stopped. Its tracks dug into the concrete. The loader's bucket buckled in on itself.
For a moment the two machines sat face to face in a cloud of grey dust, like rival bulls on a dusty plain. Then the Komatsu's cab door, a welded block of steel with a cracked lexan window, swung slowly sideways, and a single barrel came out through the nearest port.
The shots came in a clean, rapid stutter. Shattering the loader's cab glass. Punching holes through the yellow hood. The driver ducked and rolled out the far side and ran for the silo steps with his arms over his head.
Siddharth Mondal screamed something and threw his phone at the nearest truck. It bounced off the grille and skittered into the gutter.
The Komatsu turned.
Its blade came down. It caught the batching silo's lower legs and pushed, and the entire tower of steel and concrete shuddered and leaned. Then it toppled, slow as a sleeping elephant rolling over, and crashed onto the apron in a cloud of pulverised cement and a long, dismal shriek of tearing metal.
Siddharth Mondal ran.
He got fifty metres before the Komatsu's rear-facing .50-calibre rifle opened up. The sound was not like anything in the district. It was a heavy, thudding roar that went on and on, each round kicking the air out of Asansol's afternoon, and the wall of the plant office disintegrated into bricks and dust and paper. Mondal went down on the asphalt, flat, with his hands over his ears.
He lived. He would spend three weeks in the hospital in Burnpur and emerge with a limp and a hearing loss in his left ear that he would never fully lose.
But the batching plant was gone.
---
Two kilometres away, at the District Library on Station Road, the children's story hour had just begun.
Twenty-two children sat cross-legged on a rug in the reading room, their school bags stacked against the wall, listening to a retired schoolteacher read aloud from a picture book about a crow and a clay pot. The room was cool. The fans turned slowly overhead. Through the tall windows the afternoon heat shimmered over the road.
The first thing they heard was the rumble. It came up through the floor like a train passing underground, and the sound of it made the window glass hum in its frames.
The schoolteacher stopped reading. She looked up.
The second thing was the scream of a siren, far off, rising and falling, and then the crash of a wall falling somewhere outside, and then a long, grinding shriek, close now, and the library's west wall shuddered visibly, the plaster cracking in a thin white line from floor to ceiling.
"Bahar jao," said the schoolteacher, very calmly, standing up. "Out. Now. The side door. Walk. Don't run. Take your bags."
The children stood. A few began to cry. The youngest boy, four years old, stood frozen with the crow's picture book open in his hands. The schoolteacher went to him, knelt, took the book from his fingers, and lifted him onto her hip.
"We're going to play a game," she said. "Who can get to the side door first without looking back?"
They ran. They ran with their bags banging against their backs and their shoes slapping the floor, a stampede of small bodies squeezing through the side door as the west wall bowed inward behind them. The last child out was a girl of nine, pale and silent, who turned on the threshold to look back and was seized by a parent and dragged clear just as the wall went down.
The schoolteacher came out last. She was carrying the four-year-old. The library's west wall fell into the reading room in a slow, roaring avalanche of brick and books and tin shelving, and the rug where the children had been sitting vanished under a heap of splintered wood and paper.
The timing was sixty seconds. Nobody in the room had had more than that.
---
At Lahiri house on Station Road, three doors down from the library, Mrs. Anima Lahiri was sitting in her wicker chair on the veranda, eighty-two years old, a white shawl on her shoulders, a glass of tea going cold on the table beside her. She had been the wife of the municipal chairman for thirty-one years. She had kept his correspondence in tin boxes under the bed. She had kept, in a separate box, a folder of papers she had never shown anybody.
She heard the rumble. She heard the library fall. She did not get up.
When the Komatsu came down the lane, its blade swinging in low arcs across the road, and its treads chewing up the pale brick pavers of the veranda steps, she sat still and looked at it with a faint, grave curiosity, as though a visitor had come to call on the wrong day.
Then the veranda collapsed.
The machine turned, rolled sideways, and drove its front blade into the wall of the house, and the roof came in slowly from the back, sagging toward the wicker chair like a tired man lowering his head.
A neighbour's son, a boy of sixteen named Partha, was on the far side of the lane with a camera he had bought from a shop on Bus Stand Road. He pressed the shutter button six times. Later, the photographs would be published in the local daily, under a headline that said WIDOW SURVIVES AS CHAIRMAN'S HOUSE FALLS.
Partha ran across the road. He ran into the wreckage, past the bent iron gate, into the splintered drawing room, shouting her name. He found her under the back window, where the roof had caught on a broken beam and held. She was still in the wicker chair, though it had tipped over sideways. Her shawl was torn. There was a cut across her forehead, bleeding freely.
"Mrs. Lahiri," Partha gasped. "Come. Come now."
She looked at him. Her eyes were perfectly clear.
"Beta," she said, in a voice like paper, "that machine is not looking for me. It is looking for the box in the study. You will find the study. You will take the box to the newspaper. You will not open it on the road."
Partha did not understand. He pulled her out through the back window while the front of the house ground and sagged behind them, and carried her across the garden to the neighbour's wall, where a crowd of women were screaming, and laid her down under a shade tree.
When he looked back, the study was gone.
---
By then the police had come.
Asansol's police station on Ukhra Road had sent two jeeps, then a third, then a truck full of constables with rifles and shotguns and a rolled-up cloth banner marked LAW AND ORDER that nobody thought to unfurl. The first officers to reach the Grand Trunk Road fired into the machine's flanks at close range, a volley of rifle rounds that struck the tool-steel plate with a noise like hailstones on a tin roof. Sparks leapt off the surface. The rounds did not penetrate. They left white scars that the rain would never wash away, and the Komatsu kept rolling.
Two hundred and forty rounds, the report later said. Most struck the armour. Some struck the lexan. A few punched through the rubber gaskets around the ports, and the cameras shattered, and the feeds went black. Inside, Pratap Banerjee sat calmly in his cab with the monitor dark and the air conditioner humming and the blue glow of the control console on his face, and he listened to the hammer of bullets against the walls and did not flinch once.
Inspector Dhar of the Asansol police climbed the hull of the Komatsu at a quarter past three, with a rope tied around his waist and a revolver in his belt. He reached the top of the cab. He lay flat on the warm steel and fired six rounds from a service carbine into the cooling-fan port on the engine housing, and the port cover ricocheted off into the road in a shower of sparks.
The machine did not stop. It shook him off like a dog shaking off water, and he slid down the flank of the hull and fell into the gutter, bleeding from a deep cut on his shin.
At half past three a constable climbed onto the Komatsu's rear deck with a flashbang grenade. He dropped it down the exhaust stack. The blast was muffled and enormous, and a plume of white smoke belched from the stack and rolled across the road, and the engine coughed. The machine rolled on.
In Kolkata, the Chief Minister's office was on the phone to the Union Home Ministry. A conversation about deploying an armed helicopter was being drafted on a yellow legal pad by a deputy secretary who had never seen Asansol before and never would again. The helicopter, if it came, would have a missile on each side of its fuselage, rated to destroy a hardened structure at a range of six kilometres.
It was never needed.

Chapter 10: The Hardware Pit
The Komatsu went east at 3:40, away from the library and the wreck of Lahiri house, down a long curving lane of shuttered shops toward the old commercial heart of Asansol. It moved slowly, its tracks grinding the tarmac, and every shop it passed had its shutter pulled down and its owner standing behind it, peering through a crack.
It came to Farooq Hardware Supplies at 3:55.
Farooq's shop stood on a corner, a single-storey building of yellow brick with a wide front window full of paint tins and garden hoses, and a basement loading bay beneath it, reached by a steep concrete ramp from the side lane. The basement held three tonnes of cement, steel rebar, and a stack of cast-iron sewer pipe that had been ordered for the municipality and never collected. The pipe was the same pipe that had been installed, badly, under Grand Trunk Road eleven years earlier, and had cracked in the second monsoon.
Pratap Banerjee had dealt with Farooq for twenty years. The old man had once refused to sell him a box of bolts on credit, and had then, the following month, quietly paid for the funeral of Pratap's mother when the family could not afford the cremation fee. It did not make any difference. In the list, under the glass paperweight, Farooq's name had a Yes beside it.
The Komatsu came down the lane at a walking pace. Its blade swung wide and smashed through the shop's front window in a single slow sweep, bringing down a glittering curtain of glass and a fall of paint tins that split open and ran in long rivers of colour across the floor. Red, blue, white, yellow, pooling in a thick sticky swirl.
Farooq himself came running out of the back office in a white vest and dhoti, his grey hair sticking up in tufts, shouting, and got to the front door just as the machine turned its rear toward the shop, and the rear wall of the building came in.
He did not get out.
The Komatsu's right track rolled backward, straight over the lip of the basement ramp.
It went down slowly, the way a boat goes down a slipway, groaning and shrieking, its weight pressing on the concrete ramp until the steel cracked. The track slid over the edge into the basement and sank. Tonnes of cement and steel rebar and cast-iron pipe shifted beneath it, and the whole loading bay gave way with a noise like a mountain splitting. The machine lurched sideways. Its left track held the ground. Its right track sank into the rubble up to the axle, and the hull tilted, grinding, until the front blade hit the far wall of the basement and stopped, and the engine's roar climbed to a high, straining howl.
It could not move.
Inside the cab, Pratap Banerjee leaned forward, his hand on the throttle, watching the monitor's dead screen. He did not need the cameras. He could feel the machine. He could feel the hot strain in the engine and the wrongness of the tilt, the slow, sick weight of the track in the pit.
He pushed the throttle further. The engine screamed. The right track spun in the rubble, throwing up a shower of pipe fragments and grit, and went nowhere.
Outside, Supervisor Biswas of the Asansol Municipal Public Works Department had been standing in the lane for twenty minutes with a crowd of shopkeepers and a clipboard. He had watched the Komatsu come through the window of Farooq's shop with a look of professional disgust, as though somebody had driven a stake through a building he had personally inspected. Now he turned to a driver standing beside a yellow road scraper parked at the kerb.
"Start it," he said. "Pin the back."
The road scraper was a monstrous thing, a flat steel blade the width of two cars mounted on a massive chassis, built to flatten the dust of the highways in summer. The driver climbed into its cab and fired its engine. The blade came down with a clang onto the Komatsu's rear deck, and then the scraper pushed, its wheels biting the tarmac, its blade grinding into the armour plate with a shriek of tool steel scraping tool steel.
The Komatsu's rear hull buckled. The engine howled. The machine sank another foot into the pit.
A thin column of black smoke rose from the engine housing, then thickened. Oil dripped from the chassis and hit the hot basement floor with a faint sizzle. The air filled with a sharp, bitter stink of burnt grease and molten rubber.
The engine coughed.
It coughed again, a deep, broken sound, and then it died.
---
At 4:10 in the afternoon, the Komatsu D355A was silent for the first time since 2:05.
The crowd in the lane held its breath. Somewhere up the road a dog barked, then stopped. The smoke drifted lazily from the engine's exhaust stack and spread across the shattered shopfronts in a dark, greasy veil. The lane smelled of cement and paint and hot metal.
A police captain walked slowly down the middle of the road with his service pistol drawn and his cap in his other hand. He stopped twenty metres from the machine's front blade. He looked at the cracked lexan ports, the bulging steel hull, the scarred blue-black seam where the hatch had been welded shut.
"Banerjee," he called, in Bengali, hoarse. "Come out. Come out with your hands up. It's over."
Silence from inside the hull.
"Banerjee. You have a family in Kharagpur. Your brother is waiting. Come out and talk to him."
Nothing.
The captain lowered his pistol. He turned to the supervisor, who was standing at the edge of the rubble with his clipboard against his chest. "Can we cut it open?"
"Not with this," the supervisor said. He looked up at the great steel shell, its tracks sunk in the basement, its blade buried in Farooq's cement, and his face was grey. "Nothing here cuts it. We'd need a torch rig from Durgapur, and a crane, and three days. He welded the hatch from inside."
They waited.
At 4:30, a single sound came from inside the Komatsu. It was not loud. It was not the roar of the rifle or the boom of the 50-calibre or the rattle of the sheet-iron walls. It was a short, flat, distant report, muffled by thick steel and kept inside the sealed hull, and it was the last sound that Asansol heard from the machine that day.
The crowd did not move. The captain did not raise his pistol. He stood in the lane with his cap in his hand and looked at the smoking mass of tool steel and concrete and failed engine, and he thought, the supervisor would later say, about a welder's hands, and the cheque with one word written across it, and the years of his own family's dull, obedient life in a town that had never once been asked what it wanted.
Nobody went in. Not that night. The army came up from Panagarh at dawn, with a crane.
By then the thing inside had cooled, and nobody could say for certain what the cameras had recorded on the last tape, the one in the cab's recorder that had been running since 2:05, because the tape was never found.
